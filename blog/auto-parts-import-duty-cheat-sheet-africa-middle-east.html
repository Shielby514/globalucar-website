Auto Parts Import Duty Cheat Sheet: Kenya, Nigeria, Ghana, UAE & Saudi Arabia

📅 May 19, 2026 ⏱ 11 min read ✍️ Globalucar Editorial
Quick Answer: Comprehensive import duty reference for auto parts buyers in Kenya, Nigeria, Ghana, UAE, and Saudi Arabia. Covers HS codes, tariff rates, VAT, pre-shipment inspection requirements, and total landed cost calculations with real FOB Guangzhou price examples.

Why Import Duty Knowledge Matters More Than Price

Every experienced auto parts importer has the same story: they found a great supplier on Alibaba or at the Canton Fair, negotiated a low FOB price, placed the order — and then discovered that customs duties, taxes, and port charges added 30–55% to the final cost. In some cases, the total landed cost of a "cheap" Chinese part exceeds the price of buying from a local distributor.

Understanding import duty structures before you place an order is not optional — it is the foundation of profitable importing. This guide provides current (as of May 2026) duty rates, HS code classifications, and total cost calculations for five of the most important auto parts import markets in Africa and the Middle East: Kenya, Nigeria, Ghana, UAE, and Saudi Arabia.

All duty rates in this guide are compiled from official government customs tariff publications and verified against Kingmay Auto Parts' shipping records for containers delivered to these markets in 2025–2026. Rates change periodically — always confirm with your local customs broker before placing a large order.

How to Use This Guide: The Landed Cost Formula

The price you negotiate with your Chinese supplier is the FOB (Free on Board) price — it covers the goods and getting them onto the ship at the port of origin (Guangzhou Nansha Port). Your actual total cost includes several additional layers:

Total Landed Cost = FOB Price + Ocean Freight + Insurance + Import Duty + VAT/Local Tax + Port Handling + inland Transport

For quick estimation, most auto parts importers in Africa and the Middle East calculate a cost multiplier — the factor you multiply the FOB price by to estimate your total landed cost at the warehouse door. Typical multipliers range from 1.25× to 1.65× depending on the destination country and product category.

Throughout this guide, we provide worked examples using real FOB prices from Kingmay's catalogue. These examples show you exactly what a container of brake pads, filters, or ball joints will cost you at the warehouse — not just at the factory gate.

Kenya — Auto Parts Import Duty and Requirements

Kenya is East Africa's largest auto parts import hub, serving both the domestic market and landlocked neighbouring countries (Uganda, Rwanda, South Sudan, eastern DRC) through the Mombasa port corridor. The Kenya Revenue Authority (KRA) administers import duties through the East African Community (EAC) Common External Tariff (CET).

Cost ComponentRate / AmountNotes
Import Duty (CET)25% (most parts) or 0% (specific items)Brake pads, filters, chassis parts: 25%. Some electrical components: 10%. Pure lubricants: 10%.
VAT16%Calculated on (CIF value + duty). Standard rate applies to all auto parts.
Railway Development Levy (RDL)2%Applied on CIF value. Funds the Standard Gauge Railway project.
Import Declaration Fee (IDF)3.5% of CIFCapped at KES 500,000 (approx. USD 3,850) per consignment.
KEBS Pre-VerificationUSD 200–500 per consignmentPre-Export Verification of Conformity (PVoC) required. Certificates issued by appointed agencies.

Worked example — Brake pad set for Toyota Corolla:

  • FOB Guangzhou: USD 8.00
  • Ocean freight (Mombasa): USD 1.20
  • Insurance: USD 0.10
  • CIF value: USD 9.30
  • Import duty (25%): USD 2.33
  • RDL (2%): USD 0.19
  • IDF (3.5%): USD 0.33
  • VAT (16% on CIF + duty + RDL + IDF): USD 2.00
  • Total landed cost: USD 14.15 (multiplier: 1.77×)

Kenya's total cost multiplier of 1.7–1.8× is among the highest in Africa, primarily driven by the IDF and RDL surcharges. However, Kenya's well-developed Mombasa port infrastructure and reliable customs processing make it an efficient gateway for re-export to neighbouring EAC countries.

Nigeria — Auto Parts Import Duty and Requirements

Nigeria is Africa's largest auto parts market by consumption volume. The Nigeria Customs Service (NCS) classifies auto parts under various chapters of the ECOWAS Common External Tariff (CET), with significant variations between product categories.

Cost ComponentRate / AmountNotes
Import Duty5%–20% (varies by HS code)Brake pads/linings: 10%. Spark plugs: 5%. Filters (oil/air/fuel): 10%. Shock absorbers: 10%. Ball joints: 20%.
VAT7.5%Calculated on (CIF + duty + other charges).
NITDA Levy1%National Information Technology Development Agency — applies on CIF.
CISS / Pre-shipment Inspection0.5–1.0% of FOBSGS, Bureau Veritas, or Intertek. Risk Assessment Report (RAR) required.
Port charges (Lagos Tin Can/Apapa)VariableExpect USD 400–800 in terminal handling, documentation, and demurrage risk.

Worked example — Ball joint for Toyota HiLux (lower):

  • FOB Guangzhou: USD 8.00
  • Ocean freight (Lagos): USD 1.50
  • Insurance: USD 0.10
  • CIF value: USD 9.60
  • Import duty (20%): USD 1.92
  • NITDA (1%): USD 0.10
  • CISS (0.75% of FOB): USD 0.06
  • VAT (7.5% on sum): USD 0.89
  • Total landed cost: USD 12.57 (multiplier: 1.57×)

Nigeria's duty structure is generally more favourable than Kenya's for individual parts, but port congestion at Lagos (Apapa and Tin Can) adds unpredictable delays of 7–21 days and potential demurrage charges. Importers budget for an additional 5–10% contingency above the calculated duty costs to cover port delays and logistics variability. HS code classification is critical in Nigeria — the same part can attract 5% or 20% duty depending on which code the customs officer assigns.

Ghana — Auto Parts Import Duty and Requirements

Ghana's auto parts import regime operates under the ECOWAS CET framework, similar to Nigeria but with generally lower surcharges and a more streamlined customs process at the Tema port.

Cost ComponentRate / AmountNotes
Import Duty (ECOWAS CET)10%–20%Most brake, chassis, and engine parts: 10%. Some rubber/metal composite parts: 20%.
VAT15%Calculated on (CIF + duty). This includes NHIL and GETFund components.
ECOWAS Levy0.5%Community solidarity levy applied on CIF value.
Export Development and Investment Fund (EDIF)0.5%Applied on CIF value.
GCNet / Pre-shipment inspectionUSD 100–300 per consignmentGhana Community Network Services processes advance entry declarations.

Worked example — Oil filter for Toyota Corolla:

  • FOB Guangzhou: USD 1.50
  • Ocean freight (Tema): USD 0.40
  • Insurance: USD 0.02
  • CIF value: USD 1.92
  • Import duty (10%): USD 0.19
  • ECOWAS Levy (0.5%): USD 0.01
  • EDIF (0.5%): USD 0.01
  • VAT (15% on sum): USD 0.32
  • Total landed cost: USD 2.45 (multiplier: 1.63×)

Ghana's multiplier of ~1.6× is moderate for the West African region. Tema port has seen significant infrastructure improvements since 2019, and customs processing times have shortened to an average of 3–5 working days for clean documentation.

UAE — Auto Parts Import Duty and Requirements

The UAE (primarily Dubai and Abu Dhabi) is the Middle East's largest auto parts re-export hub, serving markets across the GCC, East Africa, and Central Asia. The UAE's duty regime is among the most straightforward in the region.

Cost ComponentRate / AmountNotes
Import Duty5%Flat rate on virtually all auto parts. One of the lowest duty rates globally.
VAT5%Calculated on (CIF + duty). Introduced January 2018.
Customs Documentation FeeAED 35–65 (USD 10–18)Per customs declaration.
Port charges (Jebel Ali)VariableHighly efficient — expect 1–3 days clearance for standard containers.

Worked example — Shock absorber for Hyundai Tucson:

  • FOB Guangzhou: USD 22.00
  • Ocean freight (Jebel Ali): USD 2.50
  • Insurance: USD 0.25
  • CIF value: USD 24.75
  • Import duty (5%): USD 1.24
  • VAT (5% on CIF + duty): USD 1.30
  • Total landed cost: USD 27.29 (multiplier: 1.24×)

The UAE's 1.2–1.3× multiplier makes it the most cost-effective destination in this guide. However, the UAE market is also the most competitive — hundreds of auto parts traders operate in Dubai's Deira and Sharjah industrial areas, and price competition is intense. The UAE works best as a re-export platform: import at low duty, add value (branding, packaging, sorting), and re-export to higher-duty African and Central Asian markets.

Saudi Arabia — Auto Parts Import Duty and Requirements

Saudi Arabia is the GCC's largest auto parts consumption market by volume, driven by a vehicle fleet exceeding 20 million units. The Saudi Customs Authority (ZATCA) administers duties through the GCC Common External Tariff.

Cost ComponentRate / AmountNotes
Import Duty (GCC CET)5%Standard rate for most auto parts under Chapter 87.
VAT15%Increased from 5% to 15% in July 2020. Calculated on (CIF + duty).
SABER / Product ComplianceUSD 100–300 per product registrationSABER platform for conformity assessment. Certificate required per product category.
Port charges (Jeddah Islamic Port)VariableClearance typically 3–5 working days. Dammam port also serves Eastern Province markets.

Worked example — Brake pad set for Toyota Land Cruiser:

  • FOB Guangzhou: USD 14.00
  • Ocean freight (Jeddah): USD 2.00
  • Insurance: USD 0.16
  • CIF value: USD 16.16
  • Import duty (5%): USD 0.81
  • VAT (15% on CIF + duty): USD 2.55
  • SABER registration (amortised): USD 0.50
  • Total landed cost: USD 20.02 (multiplier: 1.43×)

Saudi Arabia's 15% VAT — the highest in the GCC — significantly impacts total landed cost. Despite the low 5% duty, the total multiplier of ~1.4× is higher than the UAE's. Importers should also budget for SABER product registration costs, which can add USD 100–300 per product category and require factory documentation (test reports, certificates of conformity).

Practical Tips to Reduce Your Import Costs

Regardless of which market you operate in, these strategies apply to every auto parts importer:

  • Consolidate shipments: One 20-foot container holds significantly more parts by volume than weight limit allows. Maximize container utilization — aim for 18–22 cubic metres of packed goods. The ocean freight cost is largely fixed per container, so higher utilization reduces per-unit freight cost.
  • Get your HS codes right before shipping: HS code disputes at customs are the single most common cause of clearance delays. Classify your parts using the World Customs Organization's Harmonized System before you ship. Attach the HS codes to your commercial invoice and packing list. If customs disagrees with your classification, you have documentation to support your case.
  • Work with an experienced customs broker: A good customs broker in your destination country is worth 2–5% in savings through correct classification, duty exemption identification, and avoidance of unnecessary inspections. Budget USD 300–800 per container for broker fees — it pays for itself immediately.
  • Understand duty exemptions: Many countries offer reduced or zero duty for specific categories — for example, Kenya offers 0% duty on catalytic converters and some emission control components. Nigeria applies 5% duty to spark plugs instead of the standard 10–20%. Research these exemptions before ordering.
  • Factor port delays into your cash flow model: If you are selling in Lagos, budget for 7–21 additional days of inventory carrying cost compared to Dubai or Mombasa. Delays at Lagos ports can add USD 15–40 per day in demurrage charges if the container is not cleared promptly.
Golden rule: Never calculate your profit margin against the FOB price alone. Always calculate against the total landed cost. An importer who buys at USD 8 FOB and sells at USD 18 in Kenya (where landed cost is USD 14.15) is making a 27% gross margin — not the 56% margin they might assume by comparing USD 8 to USD 18.
