Hyundai Santa Fe & Kia Sorento Auto Parts Wholesale from China: Brake Pads, Suspension & Cooling — 2026 Sourcing Guide

Quick Answer: Wholesale sourcing guide for Hyundai Santa Fe and Kia Sorento auto parts — brake pads and discs, suspension and cooling — covering every generation from the Santa Fe SM (2000) to the Sorento UM (2015–2020), with verified OE cross-reference tables, FOB Guangzhou price tiers by order volume, a quality grade comparison and landed cost examples for Kenya, the UAE and Saudi Arabia.

The Santa Fe and the Sorento are the two Hyundai-Kia SUVs that appear most often on the order sheets of importers serving the Gulf, Africa and Southeast Asia. Both are large family SUVs that get used hard: seven seats filled, roof racks loaded, trailers pulled, and outside temperatures that punish every cooling loop in the engine bay. That usage pattern chews through brake pads, eats suspension bushings and keeps radiators on the wanted list year-round — which is exactly why Santa Fe and Sorento parts wholesale volume from China holds steady even when broader import demand dips.

This guide maps the generations, lists the OE numbers our order books see most for brakes, suspension and cooling, and sets out FOB Guangzhou pricing tiers, quality grade differences and the landed cost math for three reference markets. If you buy Hyundai or Kia SUV parts for resale, this is the article to keep open when your supplier asks for the next purchase order.

Why Santa Fe & Sorento Parts Never Sit on the Shelf

Three things make these two nameplates fast movers. First, the combined parc is enormous: the Santa Fe has been in continuous production since 2000 and the Sorento since 2002, across five and four generations respectively, and both were sold in volume in Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Nigeria, Kenya, South Africa, Russia and across Central Asia. Second, both sit in the 1.7–2.0 tonne class — heavy enough to wear front brake pads in 40,000–60,000 km and stress front suspension under full load. Third, in Gulf markets these SUVs run air conditioning continuously in 45 °C heat, and a marginal cooling system becomes a failed water pump or a cracked radiator tank with no warning.

For distributors the buying pattern is predictable: brake pads every month, suspension parts every quarter, cooling parts as seasonal spikes before summer. Buyers who stock the three systems together capture the full repair order instead of half of it. Our earlier guides go deeper on each system — the Hyundai-Kia brake parts comparison covers ceramic versus semi-metallic friction materials, and the summer cooling guide covers the wider hot-climate cooling catalog.

Generation Map: Order by Chassis Code, Not by Year

The single most expensive mistake in this category is ordering "2012 Santa Fe pads" without the chassis code, because generation overlaps confuse year-based ordering. Hyundai and Kia catalog these vehicles by internal platform codes, and parts pricing, fitment and even the number of piston calipers change between them.

  • Santa Fe SM (2000–2006) — first generation, 2.7 V6 petrol focus, many still running in Africa as cheap family transport.
  • Santa Fe CM (2006–2012) — the volume generation for export parts; 2.4 petrol, 2.7 V6 and 2.2 CRDi diesel variants.
  • Santa Fe DM (2013–2018) — sharper styling, Theta-II GDI engines, larger front discs.
  • Santa Fe TM (2019–2023) and MX5 (2024–) — current platforms; TM parts demand is now climbing as these vehicles exit lease fleets.
  • Sorento BL (2002–2009) — body-on-frame first generation, popular in the Gulf as a tow vehicle.
  • Sorento XM (2009–2014) and UM (2015–2020) — unibody generations that share suspension architecture with the Santa Fe DM in several assemblies.
  • Sorento MQ4 (2021–) — current generation; hybrid variants increasingly common in Gulf fleets.
Ordering rule: always specify OE number plus chassis code, and flag 2WD versus 4WD. On the Santa Fe CM and Sorento XM, the 4WD front lower control arm (54500-2P000) is a different part from the 2WD arm (54500-2B000) — mixing them in one order is the most common cross-contamination error we see from first-time buyers.

Read the table with one habit in mind: pads wear in pairs, so quote axle sets, not single corners. Material choice follows climate and customer. Ceramic compound runs quieter and produces less dust — Gulf customers with new vehicles ask for it by name. Semi-metallic handles heat cycling better on loaded diesel SUVs and costs 20–25% less, which is why fleet buyers in Africa and Central Asia standardize on it. Suspension Parts That Fail First on Loaded SUVs

Suspension wear on these platforms follows a sequence, and stocking to the sequence prevents dead inventory. Stabilizer links fail first — the small joints crack on rough roads within 60,000–80,000 km. Front shock absorbers follow, weeping oil after sustained hot, loaded running. Lower control arm bushings and ball joints degrade in parallel with the shocks, and strut mounts fail with the shocks they sit on. Rear suspension on both nameplates holds up well, but trailing arm bushings soften over time on towing Sorentos.

Two stock rules that our buyers verify the hard way. First, order control arms in LH/RH pairs — an arm-only order leaves you holding one side when the other fails a month later. Second, when shocks go, the strut mount and bearing usually go with them; a front shock order without mounts generates a second freight charge your customer did not budget for.

Material checks matter more here than on brakes because suspension parts carry load continuously. Ball joints and control arm forgings should be 40Cr or 45# steel with visible forging lines, not castings; bushings should be natural rubber for comfort markets or upgraded polyurethane where rough-road fleets dominate. Ask the supplier for batch pull-off test data on ball studs before the first container — the report separates manufacturers from resellers faster than any factory audit video.

Cooling Parts for Hot-Climate Markets

Cooling is the system where climate decides your stock list. A Santa Fe or Sorento running Gulf summers in stop-go traffic with a full load needs every watt of cooling capacity the radiator, fan and pump can deliver, and marginal parts fail exactly when the customer needs them most. The OE numbers our order books confirm for the volume generations:

  • 25310-2B100 — radiator, Santa Fe CM (2006–2012)
  • 25310-2B850 / 25310-2B970 — radiator, Santa Fe CM engine-variant applications
  • 25310-2D200 — radiator, Santa Fe DM (2013–2018)
  • 25380-2W500 — radiator cooling fan assembly, Santa Fe DM
  • 25100-27000 — water pump, Santa Fe JM/Tucson JM/Sportage II engine family
  • 21811-2B000, 21830-2B400, 21830-2B600 — engine mounts, Santa Fe CM (failed mounts starve the cooling fan drive and crack radiator tanks)

On radiator construction: the OE-standard aluminium-plastic design (aluminium core, plastic tanks) is correct for the replacement market and keeps the price point where distributors need it. Verify core thickness and row count against the application — a thin-core economizer version will pass a bench test and still overheat in a 48 °C July in Riyadh. Every radiator in a Kingmay order gets a pressure test before packing, and we recommend buyers specify the test certificate on the first order from any new supplier.

Water pumps pair with the timing service interval on the diesels — order them with gaskets and thermostat where the application allows. On the 2.2 CRDi Santa Fe, a weeping pump seal found early saves the customer a front cover job later, which is why workshops quote pumps preventively above 120,000 km.

Packing, MOQ & Container Loading

Production MOQ starts at 50 pieces per SKU — deliberately low, because a first order in this category should be a breadth order: 15–25 SKUs across pads, discs, arms, shocks, links and radiators, rather than depth on one line. That mix lets a new distributor test which applications move in their market before committing container volume.

On loading: brake parts are dense and heavy, so they ride the bottom tier; shocks and control arms pack mid-tier in individual cartons; radiators are the fragile cargo and travel top-tier in individual cartons with foam corner protection. A 20-ft container comfortably holds a mixed brake-suspension program of 8,000–10,000 pieces; swap in radiators and the count drops toward 3,500–4,500 mixed pieces. Plan LH/RH pairing on all arms and shocks, and ask for the loading plan photo set before the container gates — a supplier who photographs every tier earns the next order.

Frequently Asked Questions

What brake pads fit a Hyundai Santa Fe?

It depends on the generation. The volume applications are front pad set 58101-2BA00 for the Santa Fe CM (2006–2012), 58101-4ZA00 for the DM (2013–2018) and 58101-2WA00 for the TM (2019–2023). Order by OE number plus chassis code — never by year alone, because generation overlaps at the borders.

Are Hyundai Santa Fe and Kia Sorento parts interchangeable?

Some suspension assemblies carry across both nameplates — the front lower control arm 54500-2B000 spans the Santa Fe SM/CM and Sorento, for example — but brake pads and discs are specific to each chassis. The Santa Fe DM and Sorento XM share a caliper family, yet the pad numbers differ. Always confirm the OE number for each vehicle before assuming cross-fit.

Which suspension parts fail first on a Santa Fe or Sorento?

In order: stabilizer links (60,000–80,000 km on rough roads), front shock absorbers (weeping oil after hot, loaded running), then control arm bushings, ball joints and strut mounts. Rear trailing arm bushings soften on Sorentos used for towing. Stock the sequence, not the whole catalog, on a first order.

What is the MOQ for wholesale Santa Fe and Sorento parts from China?

Production orders start at 50 pieces per SKU with tiered pricing at 200 and 1,000 pieces. Sample batches ship in 5–7 days with no minimum beyond courier cost, and container orders run 25–35 days from deposit to port.

Do you stock radiators for Gulf-market Santa Fe and Sorento?

Yes — the 25310-series radiators for the Santa Fe CM and DM generations are standing stock items, pressure-tested before packing. Specify the engine variant when ordering, because core thickness and row count differ between petrol and diesel applications, and a thin-core version will overheat in Gulf summer temperatures.

How long does shipping take from China to Africa and the Middle East?

Sea freight from Nansha runs roughly 18–26 days to Jebel Ali and Jeddah, 25–32 days to Mombasa, and 30–40 days to West Africa, plus 5–10 days for clearing depending on the destination and documentation. Air courier is available for urgent sample and top-up orders at courier rates.
