Hyundai & Kia Clock Spring (93490): Why the Horn and Airbag Light Fail Together

📅 2026-11-23 ⏱ 11 min read ✍️ Author: Shirley | Senior Autoparts Procurement Specialist

On a Hyundai or Kia, the horn, the driver airbag and every button on the steering wheel pass through one rotating joint, and the part that survives that joint is a coiled flat ribbon inside a plastic drum. The catalogue calls it CONTACT ASSY-CLOCK SPRING, the number starts with 93490, and workshops call it a clock spring. When the horn dies, the airbag light comes on and the volume buttons quit at quarter turn, it is the first component worth testing and usually the last one tested.

What the clock spring carries, and the five symptoms that point at it

A clock spring is a slip ring built from a flat ribbon cable rather than brushes. The ribbon is wound several turns in one direction so the wheel can rotate about 2.5 turns lock to lock without stretching a conductor. Three to six tracks run inside it, each a separate circuit with its own failure mode, which is why a clock spring rarely dies completely — it dies one track at a time, and the order of death is what confuses diagnosis.

The last row is not a manufacturing defect. A clock spring ships locked at centre with a tab or screw holding the drum. If a technician turns the drum by hand while the wheel is off — to line up a mark, to reach a connector, out of habit — the ribbon loses its centre reference, still passes a bench continuity test, and tears itself apart within a week of driving. No warranty covers that.

Workshop rule: with the wheel off, tape the drum or refit the locking screw before anything else. If the drum has already been turned and you cannot establish its centre, treat the unit as scrap. A wrong centre destroys the ribbon in a few hundred kilometres and takes the airbag circuit with it.

Reading the 93490 prefix: how Hyundai and Kia number the spiral cable

Hyundai and Kia share the numbering convention, which is why one reference often serves both brands. Group 93490 means contact assembly, clock spring. The five characters after the dash are a platform and variant code, not a serial: the first two identify the column family, the last three distinguish the wheel fitted to it — paddle shifters or not, heated wheel or not, 12-pin or 20-pin connector body.

Our OE master file holds 236,445 reference rows, of which 174 are unique references under 93490. The distribution shows where volume sits: families 1R and 2M carry eight references each, family 29 seven, family 3V six, and families 2B, 2C and 34 five each. Everything else is three or four deep.

Two habits keep a parts counter out of trouble. Quote from the VIN or the registration, never from the model year, because Korean manufacturers changed wheel suppliers mid-cycle and a 2019 car built in two plants can carry two different drums. Then ask for a photo of the label on the old unit; it settles in ten seconds what a catalogue argument cannot settle in ten minutes. If the label is gone, the pin count in the connector body is the next identifier, and our OE number search returns the families that share it.

Bench test in eight minutes: resistance, continuity and the airbag squib

Most workshops replace a clock spring on suspicion because testing it looks risky. It is not risky if the airbag system is de-energised properly, and it takes less time than the warranty claim on a part that turns out to be fine.

  1. Disconnect the battery negative and wait three minutes — the airbag module holds enough charge to fire a squib for a short period. Five minutes is better. Pull the horn fuse too.
  2. Remove the airbag module and unplug the yellow connector. Never probe the inflator side with a powered meter; every measurement below is taken on the clock spring side.
  3. Measure the horn tracks. Under 1 Ω between the horn pins, steady through the drum's full travel. Any jump above 5 Ω is a failing ribbon, not a dirty contact.
  4. Measure the squib tracks. A healthy driver airbag circuit reads close to 2 Ω at the connector, and most manufacturers specify a window between 1.4 and 2.6 Ω. An open circuit here is the classic failed clock spring signature.
  5. Wiggle while watching. Rotate the drum 30 degrees each way with the probes on. Stable at centre and unstable off centre means a partially broken ribbon — exactly the failure that returns as an intermittent airbag light.
  6. Read the code block, not just the code. Hyundai and Kia airbag faults sit in the B1xxx range, with driver airbag resistance codes (B1346 and its neighbours) naming the squib circuit. A code naming the driver airbag plus a squib reading near 2 Ω means the clock spring is fine and the fault is in the module or harness.
  7. Refit with the drum locked at centre and confirm lock to lock turns freely before the battery goes back on.

One failure the meter cannot see is a ribbon that is intact but wrongly centred. Continuity is perfect through the middle of the travel and the part still tears itself apart in service, which is why the mechanical check matters as much as the electrical one: count the turns available each way from the drum's rest position and compare against the lock-to-lock figure for the model.

Three quality tiers, and the one that fails in the first summer

Clock springs are a low-price part with a high return cost, which makes them a favourite target for cost-cutting. The differences are invisible from outside — same housing, same connector, same label position. They sit in the ribbon's conductor alloy, in whether the drum is greased with a silicone compound that stays put at 70 °C, and in whether the pins are brass or tin-plated steel. A cabin parked in Gulf sun passes 70 °C, and a marginal ribbon hardens and cracks by August.

We sell the middle tier and describe it as what it is: an export OE-grade unit built to the Korean drawing by a supplier who also runs Tier-1 shifts, not a part out of a Hyundai or Kia box. Buyers who need the original-fit tier for a warranty repair should hear the difference before they order, because the price gap is real and so is the life gap. The budget tier is not worthless for a high-mileage taxi in a temperate market, but it should never go into a fleet vehicle working in 45 °C heat, where the comeback costs three times the part.

Failure patterns by market, and how to stock for them

The same part fails differently depending on where the car lives. In the Gulf and Saudi Arabia, heat and UV do the damage: the ribbon hardens, the grease migrates, and failures cluster on cars parked outside. In West and East Africa, dust works past the drum seal and rough roads add steering cycles — a Lagos or Nairobi taxi turns the wheel far more per kilometre than a highway car. In Central Asia, cold embrittlement splits a ribbon already heat-cycled, and the failure arrives as a sudden open circuit rather than an intermittent one. Iran is a case of its own: with a large parc of older Hyundai and Kia models and limited access to new original parts, clock springs are a consumable, and demand is spread across families rather than concentrated in the newest.

Ordering, packing, and the questions buyers ask most

Most questions that reach us about clock spring orders are not about the part. They are about identification, substitution, and what happens when a unit fails early.

How do I know which 93490 reference a car needs?
Take the VIN, or the label on the old unit. Model year alone is unreliable because wheel suppliers changed mid-cycle. If the label is gone, a photo of the connector body narrows it to one or two families and we confirm before quoting.

Can 93490-1R410 replace 93490-1R440?
No. References inside a family share a housing and ribbon width but differ in pin count and button layout. Fitting the wrong one leaves buttons dead or forces the connector, which bends pins and creates an intermittent airbag fault harder to find than the original problem.

Is the airbag light always the clock spring?
No, and that is where parts get sold unnecessarily. Read the code block first. A driver airbag resistance code with a squib reading near 2 Ω points at the module, the harness or the seat and belt circuits instead.

What makes a new clock spring fail within a month?
Almost always a drum turned with the wheel off, or a centre lock released in the wrong order. It is installation, not the part, and it is visible in the ribbon pattern when the unit is opened. We ask for the failed unit back before crediting.

Can you supply references outside your list?
Yes. We hold 174 references under 93490 and source beyond that against a VIN or label photo, usually in 18 to 25 working days. Our parts finder groups references by system so you can see what sits alongside a given drum.

How does this fit a steering order?
Clock springs fail alongside the parts that share the column and front end, and buyers consolidating a shipment add tie rod ends, rack boots and column switches to the same carton. Our guide to Hyundai and Kia steering and suspension parts covers that side, and the electrical parts guide covers the circuits sharing the same connectors.

If you stock for a market where clock springs move steadily, start with three references from your two deepest families plus the single-reference families that cover the widest model spread. Send us the VIN list you actually see in the workshop and we will build the mix from it. Quotes come back with the plating specification stated, the carton quantity, and the locking-tab check written into the packing note.
